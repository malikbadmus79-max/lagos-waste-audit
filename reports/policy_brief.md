# Paid but Not Served: Fixing Household Waste Collection in Lagos

Policy brief, October 2026. Prepared from the analysis in this repository. Source IDs refer to `docs/sources.md`, conflict IDs to `docs/data_conflicts.md` and assumption IDs to `docs/assumptions.md`.

## Summary

Household waste collection in Lagos is failing in ways that the official figures do not show. Reported disposal tonnage is estimated from truck trips and is inconsistent with stated collection capacity. In a pilot survey, households that pay their waste bill regularly were almost as likely to go more than two weeks without collection as households that do not pay. The clearest pattern is by road condition: households on untarred roads waited longest. Disposal sites at the end of their operating lives add delay. The evidence points to service delivery (access, disposal turnaround and accountability) rather than resident non-payment as the main problem.

## The problem

Lagos generates an estimated 13,000 to 15,000 tonnes of household waste per day [S03, S06, S09]. In June 2026, refuse heaps on roads, markets and residential streets prompted an emergency evacuation order from the Governor [S25], followed by a sustained run of news coverage. Official statements most often attribute the problem to residents who dump waste or do not pay their bills.

## What the evidence shows

1. Official tonnage cannot be used to monitor service. LAWMA reported 418,500 tonnes reaching disposal sites in May 2026 [S01], which is 2.7 to 3.4 times the 4,000 to 5,000 tonnes per day that LAWMA stated PSP operators can collect [S06]. Reported tonnage equals exactly 10 tonnes per truck trip in separate figures [S02, A02], which indicates that it is estimated from trip counts rather than weighed. Reported volumes fell by 80% between May and late July 2026 (C03).

2. Paying households are not reliably served. Of 13 surveyed households that pay regularly and use the PSP or LAWMA truck, 6 had last been served more than 14 days earlier, against 3 of 5 households that pay sometimes or not at all [S17]. None of the non-paying households cited cost; two cited poor service.

3. Road access separates served from unserved households. Six of 8 surveyed households on untarred roads had waited more than 14 days, against 3 of 10 on good tarred roads [S17]. LAWMA has begun tricycle collection for communities that compactor trucks cannot reach [S24].

4. Disposal sites slow collection. Queues and access problems at disposal sites appear in 7 of 21 news reports of service failure. Olusosun and Solous were described in July 2026 as at the end of their operating lives [S24], and the Governor's wife told residents in September 2026 that trucks queue for two to three days before they can tip at disposal sites [S26]. Surveyed households with long gaps live in LGAs farther from an active site [A09].

5. Operator coverage is uneven. The January 2024 PSP directory lists one operator per 3,835 residents in Eti-Osa and one per 40,430 in Ajeromi-Ifelodun and 45,434 in Epe [S18, S19]. Operator counts did not predict where failure was reported, but the spread raises a question of equity in how wards are allocated.

## Recommendations

1. Publish weighed, monthly operational data. LAWMA should publish monthly tonnage from weighbridge records at each disposal site and transfer station, alongside truck trips, so that reported volumes can be checked against capacity. Trip counts multiplied by a fixed load are not a measure of collection.

2. Measure missed collection where residents live. LAWMA already receives complaints by toll-free line and social media and rates PSP operators on a performance matrix [S24]. A ward-level record of missed collections, published quarterly, would show where service fails and would make the performance matrix verifiable.

3. Plan collection for streets that compactors cannot reach. Untarred and narrow streets need a scheduled service using tricycles or small vehicles, with fixed days and a shared collection point, rather than ad hoc arrangements that leave residents paying cart pushers. Ward allocations should identify these streets explicitly.

4. Cut disposal turnaround before the new transfer stations open. Until the Olusosun transfer loading station and the planned Ikorodu facility operate [S23], truck queuing time at Olusosun, Solous and Ewu-Elepe should be recorded daily and reduced through extended opening hours and separate queues for compactors.

5. Link fee enforcement to a service standard. Enforcement against non-paying residents is unlikely to improve service where paying households also go unserved. A published minimum standard, such as collection at least every two weeks, would give residents and LAWMA a common basis for billing disputes and operator sanctions.

6. Review operator allocation across LGAs. LGAs with the most residents per operator (Epe, Ajeromi-Ifelodun, Badagry, Ojo and Mushin) should be checked against operator fleet size and ward coverage.

## Limits of this evidence

The survey is a pilot convenience sample of 28 households from 14 LGAs, and its results show direction, not prevalence. News reports reflect where two outlets reported failure, not where failure is most common. Population figures date from 2006, and the federal and state counts differ by 8.4 million (C08). Distances to disposal sites are straight-line estimates [A09]. A larger household survey with ward-level sampling would be needed to confirm these patterns and to measure their size.
